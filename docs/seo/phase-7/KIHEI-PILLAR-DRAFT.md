## 3. Body copy (approx. 1,850 words incl. headings; trim target ≤ 1,800 at edit)

### What a Kihei chain is

A Kihei (喜平) is a flat-link chain. Each link is twisted a quarter turn and then pressed flat, so that every link lies in the same plane and the chain shows a continuous run of flat faces along its front [S3][S5]. Outside Japan the same construction is called a curb chain [S5]; in Japan the word Kihei has come to mean the whole family of these chains in gold, platinum and silver, and the vocabulary around it — cut, weave, gram weight — is how Japanese jewellers describe and price them [S3][S5][S6].

Two of our own pieces show the range. The **8C100 Necklace K18 100g 8 Cut Kihei 50cm** is a 100 g, 8-cut chain with a triple-lock clasp [S9b]. The **N1616 Necklace K18 16.65g M-Cut 16 Sided Triple Kihei 0.7mm 40cm** is a fine 16.65 g triple-weave chain at 40 cm with a fold-over clasp [S9a]. Same construction, very different presence. The rest of this guide explains the words in those two names.

### Where the name comes from

Nobody is certain. One account credits a craftsman named Kihei; another traces it to a phrase meaning a simple, pleasing shape; a third says the pattern resembles the chains on cavalry sabres [S4][S5]. What the sources agree on is that the chain style has European roots and became popular in Japan during the post-war period of rapid economic growth [S4].

### The cut: 2-cut, 6-cut, 8-cut, 12-cut and beyond

"Cut" (面, *men*) is the number of flat faces you see when you look at a link in cross-section [S3]. More faces means more small mirrors catching light, and more work at the bench.

- **2-cut** — only the top and bottom of each link are flattened. The look is plain and sturdy; retailers describe it as the most orthodox form and the one most often worn in business settings [S3][S5][S6].
- **6-cut** — top and bottom plus two more faces on each side, six in all; the surface is smoother and the shine stronger than 2-cut, and it is the most common specification on the market [S3][S5][S6].
- **8-cut** — two further faces added to the 6-cut geometry [S5]. Our 8C100 is this cut [S9b].
- **12-cut** — finer facets again, twelve faces, a denser look and a stronger sense of weight; the facets scatter light so the shine reads as fine rather than bold [S3][S5][S6].
- **20-cut and other high counts** — listed by at least one specialist retailer [S3]; rare.

Two cautions. First, **face counting is not standardised**: one maker describes a 6-cut as "two faces top and bottom, four on the sides", another counts differently, and the same chain can be labelled 8-cut by one shop and described as a 12-face triple by another [S5][S6] — so read the product photographs, not only the label. Second, names such as **M-cut** or **16-sided** on our N1616 are the maker's own designations as printed on the piece's documentation; we have found no independent definition of their geometry (**UNVERIFIED**), so we show them exactly as the maker states them and let the photographs do the rest.

The general rule retailers give is that a higher cut count takes more skill and usually costs more per gram than a plain cut of the same weight [S3][S5].

### The weave: single, double, triple

The second word on a Kihei label is the weave — how many links each link passes through [S3][S4][S5].

- **Single** — one link through one link. Light, open, with visible gaps between links.
- **Double** — one link through two. Denser, heavier-looking, with a casual solidity.
- **Triple** — three links woven together with almost no gap, so the chain reads as a flat band rather than a row of links. Triple is usually paired with higher cut counts (8 or 12) [S5], which is exactly what our N1616 is: triple weave, 16-sided cut, 0.7 mm links [S9a].

### Weight classes and how gram weight is read

Kihei chains are described by weight before anything else, and shops sort them in weight bands. One specialist's bands are **up to 19 g** ("light, everyday"), **20–30 g**, **50–60 g** ("a balanced, substantial feel") and **100 g** ("presence and impact") [S6]; another filters at under 30 g, 30–100 g and over 100 g [S3]. Within a band, the cut and weave decide how wide and how bright the chain looks.

Gram weight is the weight of the whole chain including the clasp, measured on a jewellery scale. Our listings give it to two decimal places and mark it "approx." because a fresh weighing can differ by a few hundredths of a gram [S9a][S9b]. For a chain in K18, 75% of that weight is gold and 25% is the alloy that gives the metal its hardness and colour [S7][S10]. Shops in Japan quote and buy Kihei by gram weight as the base, with the cut, weave, brand and condition adjusting from there [S5] — which is why the gram figure is in every one of our product names. How weight is measured, and what "approx." covers, is explained in the K18 guide's weight section; we do not repeat it here.

### Lengths and clasps

For necklaces, Japanese retailers describe **40–45 cm** as a clean line at the base of the neck, **50–55 cm** as the most popular length, and **60 cm** as a longer silhouette that sits on the chest [S6]; 50 cm and 60 cm are the stock lengths most often listed [S3]. Our N1616 is 40 cm [S9a] and our 8C100 is 50 cm [S9b]. Bracelets run **18–20 cm** [S6]; see our bracelets collection for current K18 chain bracelets. For how to measure yourself, use the necklace-length section of the Jewelry Guide.

Clasps on Kihei come in two main families — a spring ring (引き輪) or a box/fold-over clasp (中折れ) — and the heavier chains add one or two safety stoppers [S3]. The 8C100 uses a triple-lock clasp [S9b]; the N1616 a fold-over clasp [S9a]. The Jewelry Guide's clasps section shows each type.

### K18 vs K24 Kihei

K18 is 75% gold (18 parts in 24) alloyed with metals such as silver, copper and palladium [S7][S10]. K24 is 99.9% gold or purer [S7]. Pure gold is soft: it scratches and deforms more easily, which is why K18 — harder, and available in yellow, pink and white — is the metal most often used for rings and necklaces worn daily [S4][S5][S7]. K24 Kihei exist and some makers list them beside K18 and Pt999 chains [S6]; a buyer choosing one accepts that it will mark more readily [S5]. Cha Jewels' Kihei pieces are K18 [S9a][S9b]. For the full stamp table (K24, K18, 750, 18K, K14, K10, platinum grades), see the Jewelry Guide's Metals and stamps section — this guide does not repeat it.

### K18 hallmarks used in Japan (and the Mint mark)

Two kinds of mark can appear on a Kihei clasp tab.

The **purity stamp** — K18, or 750 in thousandths — is struck by the maker and states the gold content; K18 and 750 mean the same 75% [S5][S10]. The Japan Mint's own page is explicit that marks such as "K18" and "Pt900" are **not** the Mint's certification marks [S1].

The **Japan Mint hallmark** (品位証明記号, ホールマーク) is different. It has three parts: the Japanese flag, which shows the Japan Mint issued it; a diamond-shaped frame containing the purity in parts per thousand; and, for platinum, a platinum symbol [S1]. The Mint's grade table for gold is 999 (K24), 916 (K22), **750 (K18)**, 585 (K14), 416 (K10) and 375 (K9) [S2]. The system is **voluntary**: the Mint tests and marks pieces at the request of makers and sellers, so a genuine K18 chain may carry only the maker's K18 stamp and no Mint mark at all [S1][S3]. Custom-made Kihei in particular usually lack it [S3]. Where a piece does carry the Mint mark, our listing says so — the N1616's description states that a Mint Bureau assay mark is engraved [S9a]. Since April 2012 the Mint shows pure gold as 999 rather than 1000, and a few older grade numbers changed, so a vintage chain can carry a mark the current table does not list [S2].

One more caution from the Japanese resale trade: a stamp written "18K" (number first) is the convention of overseas and older pieces, and some buyers in Japan treat it with extra care because a minority of such pieces assay under 75% [S5]. The stamp states what the maker marked; it is not itself a test. That is why we hallmark-check every preloved piece in Japan before listing it [S10].

### Kihei for men and women

Kihei is often marketed as menswear, but the Japanese sources we checked make no gendered claim; one describes it as worn "regardless of age or gender" [S5], and our N1616 listing is written for men and women [S9a]. In practice the choice is weight and length: the under-20 g and 20–30 g bands at 40–45 cm sit close and light; 50 g and above at 50–60 cm are the chains people notice across a room [S6]. Browse by weight in the necklaces collection, or ask us for a photograph of a chain worn.

### K18 jewelry authentication in Japan

How we check a Kihei before it is listed, and how you can check one you own:

1. **Read the stamp** on the clasp tab — K18 or 750 for gold, Pt850/Pt900 for platinum [S10]. Look for the Japan Mint hallmark beside it; its absence is not a fault, its presence is a certified purity [S1].
2. **Weigh it** and compare with the stated weight; a Kihei's value is in its grams, so a listing without a weight is a listing to question [S5].
3. **Measure** the length and link width against the listing.
4. For preloved and branded pieces, Cha Jewels' hallmark check in Japan precedes listing [S10]; our FAQ explains what "preloved" means and how authenticity is handled.

A preloved example on the site is the **R7813 Ring K18/PT900 7.80g Diamond 0.172ct Soft Kihei Bee Sz# 13 [Preloved]** — a ring whose band borrows the Kihei link pattern, listed with its two metals, its gram weight and its stone weight [S9c]. (Its availability is whatever the live page shows.)

### Care

After wearing, wipe the chain with a soft cloth to remove sweat and skin oils, and store it in its case so the faces are not scratched by other pieces [S4][S5]. Hot-spring water (sulphur), shampoo and seawater can discolour or damage the surface; if a chain needs more than a wipe, wash it in lukewarm water with a little neutral detergent and a soft brush, rinse well and dry completely [S4][S5]. Avoid strong knocks: dents and deep scratches on a Kihei are visible because the faces are flat [S5]. Our Jewelry Guide's care section covers gold, platinum and pearls together; we offer polishing and repair — see the FAQ.

## 5. FAQ block (for FAQ JSON-LD; render visibly, then mark up)

1. **What is a Kihei chain?** — A flat-link chain in which each link is twisted a quarter turn and pressed flat so the faces line up; it is the Japanese name for what is called a curb chain elsewhere [S3][S5].
2. **What do 2-cut, 6-cut, 8-cut and 12-cut mean?** — The number of flat faces on each link's cross-section. More faces give a finer, brighter surface and take more work; 6-cut is the most common, 2-cut the plainest [S3][S5][S6].
3. **What is the difference between double and triple Kihei?** — The weave: double passes each link through two others, triple through three, so a triple chain is denser, flatter and almost gap-free [S3][S4][S5].
4. **How is a Kihei chain priced?** — By gram weight first, then adjusted for cut, weave, brand and condition; that is why the weight is in every product name [S5]. Our prices are shown live on each product page.
5. **Is K18 or K24 better for a Kihei?** — K18 (75% gold) is harder and the usual choice for daily wear; K24 is purer but softer and marks more easily [S5][S7].
6. **Does every K18 Kihei have the Japan Mint hallmark?** — No. The Mint's certification is voluntary and applied at a maker's or seller's request, so many genuine chains carry only the maker's K18 or 750 stamp [S1][S3]. Where a piece has the Mint mark, our listing says so.
7. **What length should I choose?** — 40–45 cm sits at the base of the neck, 50–55 cm is the most popular, 60 cm sits on the chest; bracelets run 18–20 cm [S6]. See the Jewelry Guide for how to measure.
8. **How do I care for a Kihei chain?** — Wipe after wear, store in its case, keep it out of hot-spring water, shampoo and seawater, and wash with neutral detergent only when needed [S4][S5].
